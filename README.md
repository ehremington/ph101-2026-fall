## Week 1

Welcome to Physics 1!

Physics is the study of all physical things! It is the most fundamental of all the sciences. All matter experiences *interactions* of various kinds with other matter around it and these interactions form the basis for our physical laws. The scales of matter that physicists study are vast, from the microscopic to the astronomic. All of the physical quantities we use to describe matter are taken by measurements. So each measurement needs to have a *unit* and each unit needs to belong in a system that can handle the vast range of sizes that physicists use. The *metric system* is a particularly useful one to use because changing between sizes is fairly easy to do with some practice. Since we are interested in the interactions of matter, the *relationship* of one kind of physical quantity to another and how they may change over time or position is the statement of our physical laws. Using ratios and proportionality statements is a simple but powerful way of summarizing these relationships and using them to make predictions. So this is where we will begin our journey of studying the physical world. 

After this week you can:
* describe the fundamental measurable quantities we will be studying in Physics 1
* use the metric system to convert between different scales
* use ratios and proportions to solving interesting problems involving changing quantities

Reading suggestions include sections 3, 4, and 5 of chapter 1.

## Week 2

This week we will look at *forces*. Specifically, we will consider different kinds of forces and how forces act like *vectors*. Vectors are mathematical objects with magnitude and direction. Multiple forces acting on an object at the same time act together and can be treated as a single *net force* which is what actually indicates how the motion of an object can change. The laws that describe how forces work are called Newton's laws and we will discuss them in more detail next week. For now, we just want to focus on how multiple forces on an object work. The process of vector addition of forces can be visualized graphically, but to actually compute forces we need to break them up into *components*. Doing this involves using some tools from trigonometry, so we will review and practice those skills as well.

After this week you should be able to
* discuss what is meant by a force and describe the types of everyday forces
* use the process of graphical vector addition to get an intuition about the direction of the net force
* employ trigonometry to break a force into its components that point along the x and y coordinate directions
* use the components of a force to add multiple forces together and then describe the magnitude and direction of the resulting net force.

Reading suggestions for this week are sections 1-5 of chapter 2.

## Week 3

This week we are looking at Newton's Laws of Motion. The *net force* which is the vector sum of the individual forces acting on an object determines the change in motion of an object. Newton's First Law says that if an object's motion does not change then no net force is acting on the object. If an object *accelerates* in some way (by either speeding up/slowing down or changing direction) then a net force must be causing that, and that is a statement of Newton's Second Law. Along with Newton's Laws, we will be discussing the nature of several types of everyday forces in more detail. 

After this week you should be able to:
* use vector addition to determine the net force on an object
* identify the various kinds of forces acting on an object
* apply Newton's 1st Law to determine an unknown force acting on an object
* apply Newton's 2nd Law to determine the acceleration of an object

Reading suggestions for this week include sections 6, 7, and 8 from Chapter 2 and sections 3, 4 from Chapter 3


## Week 4

This week we are taking our knowledge of Newton's second law and how the net force causes acceleration, and we are moving forward into a description of motion. This description of motion will go by the name of the kinematic equations. These equations will answer the following questions:

* Where is the object?
* Where is it going?
* How fast is it?
* When will it get there?
* How fast will it be going when it does?
* These questions all involve the quantities of position, displacement, velocity, and acceleration. We want to thoroughly understand these quantities and to do so we will employ a new graphical analysis, so that can use how one of these quantities changes over time and deduce how the others change over time. 

Further, we will consider the special case of free fall. In this case, the force of gravity is the only force acting on an object, and this will serve as a natural example of a case of constant acceleration. Situations of constant acceleration are special since they lend themselves to an easy mathematical treatment that does not require calculus to analyze.

When we do want to analyze situations of non-constant acceleration in the future, we will develop new tools and physical principles that are consistent with Newton's Laws but are more convenient to use.

This week we begin to move away from Newton's Law and begin to talk about some of the great Conservation Laws of physics. Over the next few weeks, we will discuss new the new quantities of  energy, momentum, and angular momentum  and how they are conserved within a system. A system is a collection of objects whose motion you want to study, and the surroundings are all of the things that are around your system that influence it, but you do not want to keep track of. At first we will just consider a single object as a system and this is exactly what we have been doing up to now. But we will be able to add objects to a system that interact together, and using these new conservation laws, we will be able to solve problems that would have been intractable or tedious to use Newton's Laws to analyze. 

Energy is a famously abstract quantity in physics, and simple definitions are not easy to come by. Part of this difficulty arises from the fact that energy takes many different forms, and much of the rest of this semester and next semester will be devoted to studying energy in many of its forms. As we will see, an object that is in motion has kinetic energy while an object at rest but with some height above the ground can have potential energy. More abstractly, chemical bonds that hold atoms together into molecules have a kind of potential energy, and that potential is released into motion for example when substances are burned or digested and metabolized by our bodies. Thermal energy is possessed by objects that have temperature, sound is a wave of energy transported in air pressure, light is a wave carrying energy due to its electric and magnetic fields. And as Einstein famously found in his E=mc2 mass itself is also equivalent to energy in the sense that objects at rest that have energy have mass also and vice versa.

We will first begin to grapple with what energy is by looking at one way that energy is transferred from one object to another. Work is defined in physics to be the product of the amount of force on an object that is moving by its displacement. This indicates that there is a fundamental difference in the normal force holding a book motionless on a table, and a person lifting a book with constant speed up in the air. In both cases, the force is the same as we have seen, but in the latter case something is happening to the book that makes these situations different. For a motionless book, the normal force is not doing work to the book. In the lifting case, the person is doing work to the book.

From here we should ask, well what does this work do? And the answer is that it gives the object energy.  And as a result, the object has the capacity to do work on something else.  From this place we will proceed to look into what forms of energy the book in the air can possess and how we can know that. What we will see is that energy is conserved which is to say it is not created or destroyed in any process, but can only change forms. Some forms of energy are less useful, but universally speaking energy never appears or disappears.

This week we cover another conservation law: conservation of momentum. This quantity will let us analyze multiple objects interacting and colliding with each other. In these interactions, the forces between objects may be very complex and certainly not constant, so yet again dealing with them using Newton's law is tedious if not impossible. This principle is also the best way to understand rocket ship propulsion as well as recoil from firearms. ## Week 10

This week we will be investigating rotational motion and equilibrium. There is an analogy between linear motion (also called translational motion) and rotational motion, parts of which we have already seen. In week 5 we saw that in circular motion displacement become angular displacement, velocity becomes angular velocity. We will continue this train of thought and see what other quantities we have studied in translational motion apply to rotational motion. In particular, we want to look at forces that cause rotation which are called torques. This will lead to a rotational version of Newton's second law which relates how the net torque causes angular acceleration. This will lead us into a discussion of rotational mass or rotational inertia. It turns out that the rotational equivalent to mass depends not only on the mass of the object that is spinning but also its shape. Finally we will consider an important condition known as equilibrium. This is when the net force on an object is zero and the net torque on an object is zero. This condition is important for balance, buildings, muscular forces and many many other practical applications.

After this week you can
* discuss the connection between translational motion and rotational motion
* calculate the new quantities of torque and rotational inertia and use these in Newton's second law for rotation.
* apply the conditions for equilibrium to solve for an unknown quantity

## Week 13

This week we will talk about something completely different. Well not completely completely different, but we will talk about waves. Until now, every phenomena we have considered has been about an object, which is really just a collection of particles. At first we treated the object as if all of its mass and therefore all of its particles were located at a point, and then we expanded this to consider objects like planks and rods that have extension in space. But these were essentially made of particles. Even with fluids, we thought of a fluid as a collection of very small particles that we treated as a continuous substance, but that continuous substance was moving from one place to another.

With waves, we encounter a new physical model of how matter can behave, not as particles moving from one place to another, but as a distortion or disturbance of some kind of medium that travels through the medium. Of course, the medium is made of particles, but we will focus on the disturbance of the particles moving through the medium, rather than a large scale motion of the particles themselves. We will consider waves on a string like musical instrument most heavily this week, but next week we will discuss waves in air (sound) and next semester we will discuss waves of the electromagnetic field, which is light. 

After this week you can
* discuss the nature and fundamental properties of waves with a special emphasis on periodic waves.
* calculate the speed of a wave on a string and what factors affect this speed.
* discuss what happens when waves intersect or overlap one another.
* discuss the special phenomena of standing waves.
